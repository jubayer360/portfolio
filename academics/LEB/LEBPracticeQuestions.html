Legal Environment of Business (Course Code: 04104114)

These answers are based on the course lecture materials (LEB-All). They cover the Contract Act 1872, Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, Companies Act 1994, Sale of Goods Act 1930, Carriage of Goods laws, Bankruptcy Act 1997, and Partnership Act 1932.

Note: Written in simple, easy English for exam preparation. All section numbers (Sec./Section), Act names, examples, tables, and important points are kept so you can still get full marks.

Set 1 — Answers

Question 1 (14 Marks)

a) What is meant by mercantile law? (2)
b) Discuss the main sources of mercantile law in Bangladesh. (5)
c) Explain the importance of mercantile law in today's complicated business world with suitable examples. (7)

1. a) What is meant by mercantile law? (2)

Answer: Mercantile law is also called commercial law or business law. It is the part of law that deals with the rights, duties, and conduct of people and businesses in trade and industry. It covers many legal rules about business activities. The main aim of mercantile law is to help and control business transactions by giving clear legal rules so business can run fairly and properly.

1. b) Discuss the main sources of mercantile law in Bangladesh. (5)

Answer: The mercantile law of Bangladesh has developed from several sources:

  1. English Mercantile Law: The foundation of our mercantile law is English common law and the law merchant (merchant law), which developed from the customs of merchants in England and was later absorbed into the legal system of the Indian subcontinent during British rule.
  2. Statutes (Acts of Parliament): The most important source. Major statutes include the Contract Act 1872, the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, the Sale of Goods Act 1930, the Partnership Act 1932, the Companies Act 1994, the Bankruptcy Act 1997, and the Arbitration Act 2001.
  3. Court decisions (precedents): Decisions of superior courts explain and add to statutes. Where the statute is silent, courts follow earlier judicial decisions, for example, Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903) settled that a minor's contract is void-ab-initio (void from the beginning).
  4. Customs and Usages of Trade: Established trade customs and usages that are certain, reasonable, and not against the law are recognized by courts and often clearly protected by statutes (for example, customs relating to hundis under the NI Act).
  5. Rules of fairness and justice: Where no statute, precedent, or custom applies, courts decide according to fairness and good sense.

1. c) Explain the importance of mercantile law in today's complicated business world with suitable examples. (7)

Answer: Mercantile laws play a very important role in regulating and helping economic activities. Their importance is explained below:

  1. Rules for business deals: They govern contracts, sales, leases and partnerships, so businesses can work knowing their rights and obligations are protected. Example: a supplier can enforce a supply contract against a failing buyer.
  2. Protection of rights: Contract laws ensure agreements are enforceable, while intellectual property laws protect innovations and creative works, building trust and fairness in dealings.
  3. Solving disputes: They provide methods — arbitration, mediation, and litigation — for resolving common business disputes, maintaining stability and certainty in the business environment.
  4. Consumer protection: Provisions protect consumers from fraudulent practices, misleading advertising, and low quality products, maintaining honesty in the market and consumer trust.
  5. International trade: Trade laws regulate trade between countries and resolve disputes between parties of different countries, ensuring fair commerce. Example: the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1925 governs export and import by ship.
  6. Forming and running business: They provide the framework for establishing and operating corporations, partnerships and companies — how they are formed, managed, and dissolved.
  7. Finance and investment: Regulation of banks, securities and capital markets ensures stability and protects investors, while IP laws (patents, trademarks, copyrights) encourage innovation and research investment.
  8. Growth of economy: By providing a stable and predictable legal environment, mercantile laws attract investment, encourage new businesses, help smooth working of markets, and lead to jobs and economic development.

For business graduates, therefore, mercantile law is very important for ensuring fair and lawful business practices, protecting stakeholders' interests, and helping growth in a fast-changing world.

Question 2 (14 Marks)

a) Define offer and acceptance. (2)
b) Who are competent to contract? Explain the position of a person of unsound mind with illustrations. (6)
c) "All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts." – Explain the statement in light of the Contract Act, 1872. (6)

2. a) Define offer and acceptance. (2)

Answer: According to Section 2(a) of the Contract Act 1872, a person is said to make an offer (proposal) when they show to another their willingness to do or not to do something, with a view to obtaining the agreement of that other person. According to Section 2(b), acceptance occurs when the person to whom the proposal is made shows his agreement to it; the proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise. Acceptance must be clear and without doubt and communicated to the offeror.

2. b) Who are competent to contract? Explain the position of a person of unsound mind with illustrations. (6)

Answer: As per Section 11 of the Contract Act 1872, every person is competent to contract who:

  1. is of the age of majority according to the law to which s/he is subject;
  2. is of sound mind; and
  3. is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which s/he is subject.

Position of a person of unsound mind: A person is said to be of sound mind for the purpose of making a contract if, at the time of making it, s/he is capable of understanding it and of making a proper decision as to its effect upon his/her interests.

  • A person who is usually of unsound mind but occasionally of sound mind may make a contract when s/he is of sound mind.
  • A person who is usually of sound mind but occasionally of unsound mind may not make a contract when s/he is of unsound mind.

Illustrations:

  • A patient in a lunatic asylum, who is at intervals found to be of sound mind, may contract during those intervals.
  • A sane man who is delirious from fever, or so drunk that he cannot understand the terms of a contract or form a rational judgment as to its effect on his interests, cannot contract while such delirium or drunkenness lasts.

Contracts entered into by minors or persons who cannot understand properly are void; in Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903) it was clearly decided that a minor's contract is void-ab-initio (void from the beginning).

2. c) "All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts." – Explain the statement in light of the Contract Act, 1872. (6)

Answer: Section 2(h) of the Contract Act 1872 defines a contract as "an agreement enforceable by law." So a contract has two elements: (i) an agreement, and (ii) its legal enforceability.

All contracts are agreements: Every contract always starts with an agreement — an offer by one party and its acceptance by the other. Without an agreement there can be no contract. So agreement is the base of every contract.

But all agreements are not contracts: An agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies the conditions of Section 10, that is, it is made:

  1. by the free consent of the parties;
  2. by parties competent to contract;
  3. for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object; and
  4. is not expressly declared void by law.

Agreements lacking these elements are not contracts. For example:

  • A family or social promise (for example, a father promising his son a gift for dinner attendance) lacks intention to create legal relations and is not a contract.
  • An agreement by a minor is void-ab-initio (void from the beginning) and creates no contract.
  • An agreement to do an impossible act (for example, reviving a dead person) or with an unlawful object (for example, a contract for murder) is void or illegal.
  • An agreement where terms are uncertain (for example, selling a house for a "desirable amount") is cannot be enforced, as there is no meeting of minds.

So, contract = agreement + legal enforceability; agreement is a bigger term, and only those agreements which the law will enforce are contracts.

Question 3 (14 Marks)

a) What is a negotiable instrument? (2)
b) Explain the essential characteristics of a negotiable instrument. (6)
c) Distinguish between a promissory note, a bill of exchange and a cheque with examples. (6)

3. a) What is a negotiable instrument? (2)

Answer: According to Section 13(a) of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, "Negotiable instrument means a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either to order or to bearer, whether the word 'order' or 'bearer' appear on the instrument or not." In simple words, it is a written document that promises to pay a fixed amount of money, either on demand or at a fixed future time, and can be transferred easily from one person to another. In the words of Justice Willis, "A negotiable instrument is one, the property in which is acquired by anyone who takes it in good faith and for value even though any defects of the title in the person from whom he took it."

3. b) Explain the essential characteristics of a negotiable instrument. (6)

Answer: The key characteristics of a negotiable instrument are:

  1. In writing: It must be a written document; oral promises do not qualify.
  2. Unconditional promise or order to pay: It must contain an unconditional promise (note) or order (bill/cheque) to pay; conditions destroy transferability.
  3. Fixed/certain amount: The sum payable must be certain and can be fixed easily without outside evidence.
  4. Payable on demand or at a definite time: Payment must be due either on demand or at a specific future date or determinable event.
  5. Payable to order or to bearer: If payable to order, it requires endorsement for transfer; if payable to bearer, mere delivery suffices.
  6. Free transferability: The instrument must be transferable from one party to another by delivery or by endorsement and delivery — this is its fundamental feature.
  7. Good faith acquisition (better title): A person who acquires it in good faith and for value (holder in due course (HDC)) holds it free from defects in the title of prior parties.
  8. No other undertakings or instructions: The promise or order must not be burdened with any additional undertaking.
  9. Complete and regular on its face: Erasures, alterations, or incomplete terms affect transferability.
  10. Right to sue in own name: The transferee can sue on the instrument in his own name in case of dishonor, without notifying the original debtor of the transfer.

3. c) Distinguish between a promissory note, a bill of exchange and a cheque with examples. (6)

Answer:

  • Promissory Note (Sec. 4): An instrument in writing (not being a bank-note or currency-note) containing an clear promise without condition, signed by the maker, to pay on demand or at a fixed or determinable future time a certain sum of money to, or to the order of, a certain person or the bearer. Example: A signs, "I promise to pay B or order Taka 500."
  • Bill of Exchange (Sec. 5): An instrument in writing containing an clear order without condition, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money to, or to the order of, a certain person or the bearer. Example: A creditor writes to his debtor, "Pay B or order Taka 10,000 ninety days after date," which the debtor accepts.
  • Cheque (Sec. 6): A bill of exchange drawn on a named bank and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand. Example: A draws a cheque on his bank directing it to pay Taka 5,000 to B.

Question 4 (14 Marks)

a) How is a private limited company different from a public limited company? Mention any three points of difference. (3)
b) Discuss the different clauses of the Memorandum of Association of a company. (6)
c) State the rules regarding the statutory meeting and the contents of the statutory report of a company as per the Companies Act, 1994. (5)

4. a) How is a private limited company different from a public limited company? Mention any three points of difference. (3)

Answer: Three key differences are:

  1. Listing and transfer of shares: A public limited company is listed on a recognized stock exchange and its shares can be freely transferred and publicly traded; a private limited company is not listed and transfer of its shares is completely restricted.
  2. Minimum members and directors: A public company requires a minimum of 7 members and at least 3 directors; a private company requires a minimum of 2 members and at least 2 directors.
  3. Statutory meeting and commencement: A public company must hold a statutory meeting within six months of commencement of business and requires a certificate of commencement; a private company needs no statutory meeting and can begin business immediately after registration as a company.

4. b) Discuss the different clauses of the Memorandum of Association of a company. (6)

Answer: The Memorandum of Association (MoA) is the legal document that describes the purpose for which a company is formed, defines its powers, and rules over its relations with the outside world. Any act beyond its scope is beyond power (ultra vires) and void. Its main clauses are:

  1. Name Clause: States the proposed name of the company. The name must be unique (not identical or similar to an existing company or brand), must not suggest affiliation to any religion or political organization, and must not contain indecent or obscene words. A public company adds "Limited" and a private company adds "Private Limited" at the end.
  2. Registered Office Clause: States the location and full address of the registered office, which determines the company's jurisdiction and helps communication.
  3. Objective (Objects) Clause: Limits and defines the scope of the company's operations — the main objects and the supporting objects necessary to achieve them. It protects investors' money by ensuring they are used only for the stated business purposes. It must contain nothing illegal, nothing against the rule of law or the interest of the public.
  4. Liability Clause: Explains the liability of members — limited to the face value of shares (company limited by shares) or to the guaranteed amount (company limited by guarantee). This clause is omitted in an unlimited company.
  5. Capital Clause: States the maximum capital mentioned in MoA (authorized capital) of the company, beyond which it cannot raise funds, along with the division of capital into equity and preference shares, their number and value.
  6. Association Clause: Confirms that all signatories intend to become part of the company. The MoA requires at least 7 signatories for a public company and 2 for a private company; signatures must be witnessed, and subscribers and witnesses must state their addresses and occupations.

4. c) State the rules regarding the statutory meeting and the contents of the statutory report of a company as per the Companies Act, 1994. (5)

Answer: Statutory Meeting (Sec. 83): Every company limited by shares and every company limited by guarantee having a share capital shall, within a period of not less than one month and not more than six months from the date at which it is entitled to start business, hold a general meeting of the members called the statutory meeting. The Board of Directors must forward the statutory report to every member at least 21 days before the meeting day. The report must be certified as correct by at least two directors (including the managing director, if any), then certified by the auditors as regards shares allotted, cash received and receipts and payments, and a copy delivered to the Registrar for registration. Members present may discuss any matter relating to the company's formation or arising out of the report. Default makes every director/officer in default punishable with a fine up to Taka 5,000. This section does not apply to a private company.

Contents of the Statutory Report:

  1. Total number of shares allotted (distinguishing fully/partly paid-up, allotted otherwise than in cash, and the consideration);
  2. Total cash received by the company in respect of the shares allotted;
  3. An abstract of receipts and payments up to a date within seven days of the report, receipts from shares and debentures, commission or discount paid on shares/debentures, and an estimate of preliminary expenses;
  4. Names, addresses and occupations of directors, auditors, managing agent, manager and secretary, and changes since registration as a company;
  5. Particulars of any contract to be submitted to the meeting for approval or modification;
  6. The extent of calls due from directors and the managing agent; and
  7. Particulars of any commission or brokerage paid to directors or the managing agent on the issue or sale of shares or debentures.

Question 5 (14 Marks)

a) Define partnership as per the Partnership Act, 1932. (2)
b) Identify and explain the essential features of a partnership. (5)
c) Describe the different types of partners with examples. (7)

5. a) Define partnership as per the Partnership Act, 1932. (2)

Answer: As per Section 4 of the Partnership Act 1932, "Partnership" is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Persons who enter into partnership with one another are individually called "partners", collectively "a firm", and the name under which the business is carried on is called the "firm name".

5. b) Identify and explain the essential features of a partnership. (5)

Answer: The essential features of a partnership are:

  1. Association of two or more persons: A single person cannot form a partnership; there must be at least two persons.
  2. Agreement between the partners: The relation of partnership arises from contract, not from status (Sec. 5). Members of a Hindu joint family carrying on family business, or a husband and wife carrying on business as such, are not partners by status alone.
  3. Lawful business: There must be a business undertaking or commercial activity that is lawful; partnership cannot exist for unlawful objects.
  4. Profit motive and sharing of profits: The motive must be to earn profit and share it among the partners. Sharing of profit is essential evidence of partnership.
  5. Mutual agency: The business must be carried on by all the partners or by any of them acting for all. Every partner is both a principal and an agent of the other partners — this is the true test of partnership.

5. c) Describe the different types of partners with examples. (7)

Answer: Partners are classified depending on the nature of work, extent of liability, etc. The main types are:

  1. Active/Managing (Ostensible) Partner: Takes daily participation in the conduct of the business. Example: a partner who manages the shop, deals with customers and suppliers every day.
  2. sleeping/dormant partner: Does not participate in the conduct of the business but contributes capital, shares profits and losses, and is bound by the conduct of all the partners. Example: an investor-partner who never attends to the firm's affairs.
  3. nominal partner: A partner to the firm only by name; he has no significant or real interest in the firm, contributes no capital and takes no part in management, but lends his name and reputation. Example: a celebrity whose name is used by the firm.
  4. partner in profit only: Agrees to share the profits but does not suffer losses, and is not liable for liabilities in dealings with third parties.
  5. Minor Partner: A minor cannot be a partner under the Contract Act, but under Sec. 30 he can be admitted to the benefits of partnership with the consent of all partners. He shares profits, but his liability is limited to his share in the firm — he is not personally liable for losses.
  6. Partner by Estoppel (Holding Out): A person who is not a partner but represents himself, by conduct or words, to another person as a partner cannot deny it afterwards. He becomes liable as a partner to third parties who gave credit to the firm on the faith of such representation. Example: a retired partner who allows the firm to continue using his name without public notice.

Question 6 (14 Marks)

a) Define "goods" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. (2)
b) Explain the different types of goods with appropriate examples. (6)
c) Distinguish between a sale and an agreement to sell. (6)

6. a) Define "goods" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. (2)

Answer: Under Section 2(7) of the Sale of Goods Act 1930, "goods" means every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money; and includes electricity, water, gas, stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale. Thus shares of a company and standing timber agreed to be severed are goods, whereas lottery tickets (actionable claims) and money are not.

6. b) Explain the different types of goods with appropriate examples. (6)

Answer: Goods under the Act may be of the following types:

1. Existing Goods (Sec. 6): Goods which are in the legal possession of, or owned by, the seller at the time of formation of the contract of sale. They are further divided into:

  • Specific Goods [Sec. 2(14)]: Goods "identified and agreed upon" at the time the contract is made. Example: you advertise your particular mobile phone online with its picture, and a buyer agrees to buy that phone — it is a specific good.
  • Ascertained Goods: Not defined by the Act but by judicial interpretation — specific goods which have been selected/separated from a larger set after the contract. Example: out of 500 apples, you separate 200 particular apples to sell; those 200 are identified and separated goods.
  • Unidentified and separated Goods: Goods not specifically identified, left to be selected from a larger group. Example: you agree to sell 200 apples out of your 500 without specifying which ones.

2. Future Goods [Sec. 2(6)]: Goods that will be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract of sale. A contract for future goods is always an agreement to sell, never an actual sale. Example: agreeing to sell 1,000 apples from your orchard after they ripen.

3. Contingent Goods: A subtype of future goods whose acquisition depends upon a uncertain event which may or may not happen. Example: selling apples from trees that are yet to produce apples — the sale depends on the trees actually bearing fruit.

6. c) Distinguish between a sale and an agreement to sell. (6)

Answer: Under Section 4, if the property (ownership) in the goods is transferred from the seller to the buyer under the contract, it is a sale; where the transfer of property is to take place at a future time or subject to some condition to be fulfilled, it is an agreement to sell, which becomes a sale when the time lapses or the conditions are fulfilled.

Question 7 (14 Marks)

a) Who can be declared bankrupt under the Bankruptcy Act, 1997? (3)
b) Explain the specific purposes for which the law of bankruptcy was enacted. (4)
c) Discuss the acts of bankruptcy as listed in Section 9 of the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (7)

7. a) Who can be declared bankrupt under the Bankruptcy Act, 1997? (3)

Answer: In the general sense, a man is bankrupt when he is unable to pay his debts, but he cannot legally be called bankrupt unless declared so by a competent court. Under the Bankruptcy Act 1997, any person, man or woman, who has attained majority and is not a lunatic can be declared bankrupt when:

  1. he is a debtor whose assets are insufficient to meet all his debts; and
  2. he has committed an act of bankruptcy.

Unlike the old insolvency laws (which excluded legal persons), the present Act also provides methods by which the debts of companies and other legal persons may be realized through bankruptcy law — particularly aimed at failing borrowers of banks in Bangladesh.

7. b) Explain the specific purposes for which the law of bankruptcy was enacted. (4)

Answer: The law of bankruptcy was enacted with the following specific purposes:

  1. To declare legally a debtor as bankrupt and to protect him from trouble / pressure by his creditors (recognizing that not all defaults are deliberate — failure to pay may be due to bad luck);
  2. To arrange for realization and equitable distribution of the debtor's assets among the creditors in the most quick and economical manner, so that there is no scramble over the assets and no creditor gets undue preference over others;
  3. To discharge the debtor after distribution of the assets, giving him a new start in life free from the demands of his creditors in respect of unpaid debts; and
  4. The present Act of 1997 has the further purpose of taking effective measures against failing borrowers of banks in Bangladesh, who in many cases remained outside the reach of the old insolvency law, particularly companies.

7. c) Discuss the acts of bankruptcy as listed in Section 9 of the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (7)

Answer: To initiate a bankruptcy proceeding, the person must be a debtor who has committed an act of bankruptcy. Section 9 lists the acts of bankruptcy as follows:

a) If, in Bangladesh or elsewhere, the debtor makes a transfer of all or substantially all his properties to a third person for the benefit of his creditors; b) If, in Bangladesh or elsewhere, he makes a transfer of his property or any part of it with intent to cheat or delay his creditors; c) If he makes any transfer of his property which would be void as a fraud preference under insolvency or any other law if he were declared legally bankrupt; d) If, with intent to delay or defeat his creditors, the debtor (i) departs from or remains out of Bangladesh, (ii) departs from his dwelling house or usual place of business or otherwise absents himself, (iii) secludes himself so as to deprive his creditors of the means of communicating with him, or (iv) submits by secret agreement or fraudulently to an adverse decree, judgment or order of any court or authority; e) If his property has been sold in execution of a decree for payment of money; f) If he files a plaint for being declared legally bankrupt himself; g) If he gives notice to any of his creditors that he has suspended payment of his debts; h) If he is imprisoned in execution of a decree for payment of money; and i) If one or more creditors, having a valid and debt that is due for payment of not less than Tk. 5 lacs, have served a notice demanding payment and the debtor has failed to pay within 90 days of service of the demand.

Any of these acts, coupled with the debtor's inability to pay, entitles a creditor (or the debtor himself) to initiate bankruptcy proceedings before the bankruptcy court.

Set 2 — Answers

Question 1 (14 Marks)

a) Define business law. (2)
b) Identify the scope of mercantile law, indicating the major laws it covers. (6)
c) Why should a business graduate study the legal environment of business? Explain with practical examples. (6)

1. a) Define business law. (2)

Answer: Business law (also called mercantile or commercial law) is the branch of law that deals with the rights, relations, and conduct of individuals and businesses doing business, trade, and industry. It covers the legal principles, rules and regulations related to business activities, and its main aim is to help and regulate business transactions by giving legal rules for conducting business fairly and efficiently.

1. b) Identify the scope of mercantile law, indicating the major laws it covers. (6)

Answer: Mercantile law covers many topics relating to business activities:

  1. Contracts (Contract Act 1872): Formation, interpretation and enforcement of contracts; essential elements such as offer, acceptance, consideration and legality; breach of contract and remedies.
  2. Sale of Goods (Sale of Goods Act 1930): Sale and purchase of goods — transfer of property, warranties, delivery, and conditions relating to tangible movable property.
  3. Partnerships (Partnership Act 1932): Formation, operation and dissolution of partnerships; rights and duties of partners; division of profits and losses.
  4. Company Law (Companies Act 1994): Incorporation and regulation of companies, rules for running a company properly, shareholders rights, and corporate liability.
  5. Banking, Finance and Negotiable Instruments (NI Act 1881): Banking regulations, lending practices, and financial instruments like promissory notes and cheques.

2. a) What is a quasi-contract? (2)

Answer: Sometimes rights and obligations arise not from an agreement between the parties but by law. These legal relations resemble contracts and are called quasi-contracts (like contracts created by law) (the word "quasi" meaning "seemingly"). Chapter V of the Contract Act 1872 deals with them under the heading "Certain relations resembling those created by contract", although the Act does not use the term "quasi-contract" anywhere. They rest on the principle of equity — that no one should be allowed to enrich himself unjustly at the expense of another.

2. b) Explain the different types of quasi-contractual obligations recognized under the Contract Act, 1872. (6)

Answer: Chapter V (Sections 68–72) of the Contract Act recognizes the following quasi-contractual obligations:

  1. Supply of necessaries to incapable persons (Sec. 68): If a person incapable of contracting (for example, a minor or lunatic), or anyone he is legally bound to support, is supplied with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the supplier is entitled to reimbursement from the property of such incapable person.
  2. Reimbursement of money paid which another is bound to pay (Sec. 69): A person who is interested in the payment of money which another is legally bound to pay, and who therefore pays it, is entitled to be reimbursed by the other. Example: a tenant paying arrears of government revenue to save the property from sale can recover it from the landlord.
  3. Obligation to pay for non-gratuitous acts (Sec. 70): Where a person lawfully does something for another, or delivers anything to him, not intending to do so gratuitously, and the other person enjoys the benefit, the latter is bound to compensate the former. Example: goods delivered by mistake and consumed by the recipient must be paid for.
  4. Responsibility of finder of goods (Sec. 71): A person who finds goods belonging to another and takes them into his custody is subject to the same responsibility as a bailee — he must take reasonable care of the goods and return them to the true owner.
  5. Money paid or things delivered by mistake or under coercion (Sec. 72): A person to whom money has been paid, or anything delivered, by mistake or under coercion, must repay or return it. Example: excess money paid on a bill by mistake must be refunded.

2. c) What is an illegal contract? Distinguish between void, voidable and illegal contracts with examples. (6)

Answer: Illegal contracts are contracts that violate the law of the land. Section 10 requires that the object or consideration of a contract must be lawful; thus contracts with unlawful objects are prohibited. Example: a contract for murder (contract killing) is illegal, as murder is a crime under the Penal Code.

Distinction:

  1. Void contract [Sec. 2(j)]: A contract that ceases to be enforceable by law — "void" means not legally binding. A contract becomes void when it fails the conditions of Section 10, for example, contracts by incompetent parties (minors, persons of unsound mind), or contracts whose performance becomes impossible. A void-ab-initio (void from the beginning) contract (for example, a minor's contract, per Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose) is void from the very beginning. A void contract has no legal value, but its object is not necessarily criminal, and collateral transactions are not affected.
  2. Voidable contract: A contract that can be avoided (made void) at the option of one of the parties — generally the party whose consent was not free, being obtained by coercion (Sec. 15), undue influence (Sec. 16), fraud (Sec. 17), or misrepresentation (Sec. 18). It remains valid and enforceable until the injured party chooses to cancel it. Example: a contract signed at gunpoint can be cancelled by the coerced party.
  3. Illegal contract: A contract whose object or consideration is forbidden by law — it is not only void but also criminal or immoral in nature. All illegal contracts are void, but all void contracts are not illegal. Illegality also taints collateral transactions, which become unenforceable, and parties to an illegal contract may face punishment. Example: an agreement to smuggle goods, and even a loan taken specifically to finance the smuggling, are both unenforceable.

3. c) Identify the parties to a bill of exchange and briefly explain the role of each party. (7)

Answer: The parties to a bill of exchange are:

  1. The Drawer: The person who draws (makes and signs) the bill of exchange — usually the creditor who orders payment.

  9. Acceptor for Honour: Where the original drawee refuses to accept the bill or to furnish better security when demanded by the notary, any person not already liable on the bill may, with the consent of the holder, accept it for the honour of any party liable on the bill.

4. a) Give the legal definition of a company. (2)

Answer: Legally, a company is a group of natural and artificial persons registered under the existing law of a country. Under Section 2(c) of the Companies Act 1994, "company" means a company formed and registered under this Act or an existing company; under Section 2(h), an "existing company" is one formed and registered under any earlier company law and still in operation. A registered company is regarded by law as a single person with a distinct legal personality, perpetual succession (it continues even if members change), and a common seal.

4. b) "A company is an artificial person created by law." – Explain the statement with reference to the main features of a company. (6)

Answer: A company is called an artificial person (created by law) because it is not born like a human being. It is created by law. Still, it can do many things like a person — own property, take loan, keep bank account, employ workers, make contracts, and sue or be sued. But it works only through its directors and officers. This idea is clear from the main features of a company:

  1. Separate legal person: A registered company has its own name, acts under that name, has its own seal, and its assets are separate and distinct from those of its members. It is a different "person" from the members who form it.
  2. Voluntary association: It is formed by persons coming together willingly for a lawful purpose.
  3. Limited liability: The liability of members is limited to the unpaid amount on their shares (or the guaranteed amount), protecting personal assets.
  4. Perpetual succession: Members may come and go, but the company continues to exist until wound up according to law; its life is not affected by the death or insolvency of members.
  5. Transferability of shares: Shares can be transferred, so members can change without closing the company.
  6. Separate property and capacity to sue and be sued: The company owns property in its own name and can sue and be sued in its own name.
  7. Separate management: The company is managed by directors, distinct from its owners (shareholders).
  8. Not a citizen: Although a legal person, a company is not a citizen and cannot claim citizenship rights; and its existence can be terminated only through the legal process of winding up.

Since it has legal personality but is not a natural person, and its birth (registration as a company) and death (winding up) both occur by law, a company is best described as an artificial person created by law.

4. c) Discuss the step-by-step procedure of forming and registering a company in Bangladesh. (6)

Answer: Company registration in Bangladesh is partly computerized and involves five steps:

Step 1 — Name Clearance: Generate a username on the RJSC website (www.roc.gov.bd) and apply for clearance of the proposed name, paying Taka 600 to the specified bank. The name must not be identical or too similar to an existing business name, must not infringe trademarks, and must not be obscene or already reserved. An approved name is reserved for 6 months (extendable before expiry).

Step 2 — Drafting Necessary Documents: Draft the two key constitutional documents — the Memorandum of Association (MoA) and the Articles of Association (AoA) — along with other documents required by RJSC compliance rules.

Step 3 — Opening and bringing in the paid-up investment bank account: Applicable only if there is a foreign shareholders. A bank account is opened in the proposed company's name with a scheduled bank, the foreign shareholders remits cash equal to his shares from outside Bangladesh, and the bank issues an Encashment Certificate required by RJSC.

Step 4 — Submission of Documents to RJSC for Registration: All relevant information, Form IX and the Subscriber Page are uploaded to the RJSC website; registration fees and stamp duty are paid by bank slip. On being satisfied with the documents, RJSC issues the Certificate of Incorporation.

Step 5 — Post-Registration Formalities: RJSC issues the Incorporation Certificate, Form XII (list of directors) and certified copies of the MoA and AoA. The company then obtains share certificates, registers of shareholders/managers, a company seal and rubber stamp, applies for a Trade License and Tax Identification Number, and other permits depending on the nature of its operations. Answer: A partnership deed is the document containing the terms and conditions agreed among the partners regarding the affairs of the business. 5. b) Explain the rights of partners as provided in the Partnership Act, 1932. (6)

Answer: In the absence of a deed, the Act provides the following rights:

  1. Right to determine the relationship by contract (Sec. 11): Partners may create their own terms in the partnership deed; they may even restrain one another from carrying on any business other than that of the firm.
  2. Right to take part in the conduct of business [Sec. 12(a)]: Every partner has the right to be involved in managing the firm's business.
  3. Right to express opinion [Sec. 12(c)]: All partners may freely express opinions on the firm's business; decisions on ordinary matters require the consent of other partners.
  4. Right of access to books [Sec. 12(d)]: Every partner may inspect the books of the firm.
  5. Right to share profits [Sec. 13(b)]: Partners share profits equally in the absence of an agreement.
  6. Right to interest on capital [Sec. 13(c)] and on advances: Partners may claim interest on capital (out of profits, if agreed) and on advances made to the firm beyond capital.
  7. Right to indemnity [Sec. 13(e)]: The firm must indemnify a partner for payments made and liabilities incurred in the ordinary course of business or in an emergency.
  8. Right to prevent introduction of a new partner (Sec. 31): No new partner may be introduced without the consent of every partner.
  9. Right to retire (Sec. 32): A partner may retire with the consent of the other partners, or by simple notice in a partnership at will.
  10. Right not to be expelled (Sec. 33): A partner may not be expelled except on specified grounds and only after reasonable warning and opportunity of explanation.

5. c) On what grounds may a court direct the dissolution of a partnership firm? Explain with reference to Section 44 of the Partnership Act, 1932. (6)

Answer: Section 44 provides that a partner has the right to file a suit for dissolution of the firm, and the court may direct dissolution on any of the following grounds:

  1. Unsoundness of mind of a partner — the suit may be brought by another partner or by the next friend of the partner of unsound mind;
  2. Permanent incapacity of any partner to perform his duties as a partner;
  3. Conduct prejudicial to the business — where a partner is guilty of conduct likely to affect badly / harmfully the carrying on of the firm's business;
  4. Willful or persistent breach of agreement by a partner relating to the management of the firm's affairs, or conduct making it not reasonably possible for the other partners to carry on business with him;
  5. Transfer of interest — where a partner has transferred the whole of his interest in the firm to a third person;
  6. Perpetual losses — where the business of the firm cannot be carried on except at a loss; and
  7. Just and fair ground — any other ground which renders it just and equitable that the firm be dissolved.

After dissolution, under Sec. 46, every partner is entitled, as against the other partners, to have the firm's property applied in payment of the debts and liabilities of the firm, and the surplus distributed among the partners or their representatives.

6. c) Explain future goods and depending on an uncertain event goods with appropriate examples. (5)

Answer: Future Goods [Sec. 2(6)]: Future goods are goods that will either be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract of sale. Since the goods do not exist (or are not owned by the seller) at the time of the contract, a contract for the sale of future goods can never be an actual sale — it is always an agreement to sell. Example: You own an apple orchard and agree to sell 1,000 apples to a buyer after the apples ripen. The sale is to occur in the future for goods not yet identified, so the apples are future goods.

Contingent Goods: Contingent goods are a subtype of future goods whose acquisition by the seller depends upon a uncertain event (an uncertain event) which may or may not happen. The contract contains a uncertain event clause, and its performance depends on that event. Example: You agree to sell apples from your orchard when the trees are yet to produce apples. The sale depends on the condition that the trees actually produce apples — which may not happen. The apples are depending on an uncertain event goods.

Difference: In future goods, the goods are to be produced or acquired by the seller in the ordinary course (reasonably certain); in depending on an uncertain event goods, the very availability of the goods depends on an uncertain uncertain event. In both cases the contract is an agreement to sell, not a sale.

Answer: Under Section 234, the winding up of a company may be effected in three modes:

  1. Winding up by the Court (Compulsory winding up): The court orders winding up on a petition presented by the company, any creditor, contributory, or the Registrar, on grounds specified in Sec. 241 (for example, special resolution, inability to pay debts, just and equitable grounds).   2. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930: Governs contracts for the sale of movable goods — transfer of property, conditions and warranties, and delivery.
  3. The Partnership Act, 1932: Regulates formation, operation and dissolution of partnership firms, and the rights and duties of partners.
  4. The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881: Deals with promissory notes, bills of exchange and cheques, the rights and liabilities of parties, and dishonor of instruments.
  5. The Companies Act, 1994: Governs the registration as a company, management, meetings, accounts, and winding up of companies, and rules for running a company properly.

  1. Other ways to solve disputes (ADR): Mercantile law provides for resolution of commercial disputes through arbitration, mediation and conciliation, which are faster, cheaper and more private than litigation. The Arbitration Act gives legal force to arbitration agreements and awards.

  5. International trade disputes: In a connected worldwide world, arbitration under mercantile law enables parties of different countries to resolve between countries disputes on neutral ground, so trade is conducted fairly and with confidence.

Question 2 (14 Marks)

a) What is consideration? (2)
b) Explain the essential elements of a valid consideration. (6)
c) "An agreement made without consideration is void." – Discuss the statement along with its exceptions. (6)

2. a) What is consideration? (2)

Answer: Consideration is something of value exchanged between the parties as part of an agreement — it may be money, goods, services, or a promise to do or not to do something. Under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act 1872, when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or abstain from doing something, such act, abstinence or promise is called consideration for the promise. Without consideration, a contract is generally cannot be enforced — it is the price for which the promise is bought.

2. b) Explain the essential elements of a valid consideration. (6)

Answer: The essentials of a valid consideration are:

  1. It must move at the desire of the promisor: An act done at the desire of a third party, or voluntarily, is not consideration. The act or abstinence must be at the promisor's request.
  2. It may move from the promisee or any other person: Under the Act, consideration may be furnished by the promisee or even a stranger to the consideration, so long as it is given at the promisor's desire.
  3. It may be past, present or future: Consideration may consist of something already done (past), done simultaneously with the promise (present/executed), or promised to be done in future (executory).
  4. It must be real and not illusory: Consideration must have some value in the eyes of law; a physically impossible, vague or illusory consideration (for example, a promise to "put life into a dead body") is not valid.
  5. It need not be adequate: The law requires the presence of consideration, not its adequacy. Inadequacy may, however, be evidence that consent was not free.
  6. It must be lawful: The consideration must not be forbidden by law, fraud, immoral, opposed to public policy, or such that it would defeat the provisions of any law (Sec. 23).
  7. It must be something the promisor is not already bound to do: Performance of an existing legal or contractual obligation is no consideration for a new promise.

2. c) "An agreement made without consideration is void." – Discuss the statement along with its exceptions. (6)

Answer: The general rule, embodied in Section 25 of the Contract Act 1872, is "no consideration, no contract" — an agreement made without consideration is void, because consideration is one of the essentials of a valid contract under Section 10. A bare promise (bare promise (nudum pactum)) unsupported by consideration cannot be enforced.

But, Section 25 and allied provisions recognize the following exceptions, where an agreement is valid even without consideration:

  1. Natural love and affection [Sec. 25(1)]: A written and registered agreement made on account of natural love and affection between parties standing in near relation to each other. Example: a registered deed by a father gifting property to his son.
  2. Past voluntary services [Sec. 25(2)]: A promise to compensate, wholly or in part, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do. Example: A finds B's lost wallet and returns it; B promises to pay A Tk. 1,000 — enforceable.
  3. Time-barred debt [Sec. 25(3)]: A written promise, signed by the debtor (or his authorized agent), to pay wholly or in part a debt barred by the law of limitation.
  4. Completed gifts: The rule does not affect the validity of any gift actually made between donor and donee.
  5. Agency (Sec. 185): No consideration is necessary to create a contract of agency.
  6. Charitable subscription: Where the promisee, on the faith of a promised subscription, has undertaken a liability (for example, started construction), the promise is enforceable to that extent.

So, while the general rule is that an agreement without consideration is void, these exceptions — grounded in equity, close relationships, and moral obligation — make such agreements enforceable in defined circumstances.

Question 3 (14 Marks)

a) What is meant by dishonor of a negotiable instrument? (2)
b) Explain dishonor by non-acceptance and dishonor by non-payment. Why can a promissory note or a cheque not be dishonored by non-acceptance? (6)
c) State the rules relating to notice of dishonor. When is notice of dishonor unnecessary? (6)

3. a) What is meant by dishonor of a negotiable instrument? (2)

Answer: Dishonor of a negotiable instrument refers to the state when the party who has to pay the sum on the instrument fails to honor it — that is, refuses to accept it or to pay it. It implies the loss of honor for the instrument, indicating its unsuitability to realize funds. The Negotiable Instruments Act 1881 deals with dishonor in Sections 91 to 98, recognizing two types: dishonor by non-acceptance and dishonor by non-payment.

3. b) Explain dishonor by non-acceptance and dishonor by non-payment. Why can a promissory note or a cheque not be dishonored by non-acceptance? (6)

Answer: Dishonor by Non-Acceptance: When a bill of exchange is properly presented for acceptance but is not accepted, it is dishonored by non-acceptance. This can occur when:

  1. the drawee does not accept the bill within 48 hours of showing the instrument for payment/acceptance (presentation for payment/acceptance);
  2. where there are several drawees (not being partners), even one of them defaults in acceptance;
  3. the drawee is a fictitious person, or untraceable after reasonable search;
  4. the drawee is incompetent to contract;
  5. the acceptance is qualified or conditional (the holder may treat it as dishonored);
  6. the drawee has become insolvent or is dead; or
  7. showing the instrument for payment/acceptance (presentation for payment/acceptance) is excused and the bill remains unaccepted.

Effect: On dishonor by non-acceptance, the holder gets an immediate right to sue all prior parties, even before maturity of the bill.

Dishonor by Non-Payment: Arises when the maker of a promissory note, the acceptor of a bill of exchange, or the drawee (bank) of a cheque defaults or refuses to pay the sum due when the instrument is properly presented for payment, or when it remains unpaid at or after maturity. On such dishonor, the other parties to the instrument become chargeable with liability.

Why notes and cheques cannot be dishonored by non-acceptance: Presentment for acceptance is required only in the case of bills of exchange (the drawee must accept the drawer's order before becoming liable). A promissory note contains the maker's own promise — there is no drawee to accept it; and a cheque is always payable on demand and does not require acceptance by the banker. Hence only bills of exchange can be dishonored by non-acceptance; promissory notes and cheques can be dishonored only by non-payment.

3. c) State the rules relating to notice of dishonor. When is notice of dishonor unnecessary? (6)

Answer: Rules relating to notice of dishonor:

  1. On dishonor, the holder must give notice immediately/within a reasonable time to all the parties whom he wants to make liable (the drawer and endorsers). Reasonable time depends on the distance between the parties and the nature of communication; public holidays are excluded.
  2. Except the maker and acceptor (the principal debtors), all parties to whom notice is not properly given are discharged from liability.
  3. Who may give notice: The holder, or any party liable on the instrument who wants to make prior parties liable; an agent may give notice to prior parties or to his principal. Notice by a stranger is invalid.
  4. To whom notice is given: The endorser, a properly authorized agent of a party, the legal representative of a deceased party, or the receiver/official assignee of an insolvent. No notice need be given to the maker, acceptor or drawee, as they are the principal debtors.
  5. Mode: Notice may be oral, written, or partly both; any form suffices if it informs the party (expressly or impliedly) of the dishonor; written notice may be sent by post.
  6. The object of the notice is to inform or warn the party liable — a doctrine based on justice and equity.

When notice is unnecessary (Sec. 98): No notice of dishonor is necessary —

a) when it is dispensed with by the party entitled to it; b) to charge the drawer who has countermanded payment; c) when the party charged could not suffer damage for want of notice; d) when the party entitled to notice cannot be found after due search, or the party bound to give it is unable to do so without fault of his own; e) to charge the drawer when the acceptor is also the drawer; f) in the case of a promissory note which is not negotiable; and g) when the party entitled to notice, knowing the facts, promises unconditionally to pay the amount due.

4. a) What is a share of a company? What does it indicate? (3)

Answer: A share is a unit into which the total share capital of a company is divided. Under the Companies Act 1994, the shares or other interest of any member in a company are movable property, transferable in the manner provided by the articles of the company. A share indicates:

  1. the proportion of interest/ownership of a shareholder in the company;
  2. the extent of liability — the amount, if any, unpaid on the share is the maximum the member can be called to pay; and

Answer: A member of a company is: (i) a subscriber to the memorandum of association, who is considered to have agreed to become a member and whose name is entered in the register on registration; and (ii) every other person who agrees in writing to become a member and whose name is entered in the register of members. Thus two conditions — agreement and entry in the register — make a person a member.

  1. the names, addresses and occupations of the members;
  2. in a company having share capital, a statement of the shares held by each member (showing each separately share by its number) and the amount paid or agreed to be considered as paid on the shares of each member;

  4. the date on which any person ceased to be a member.

The annual list of members:

  1. The names, addresses and occupations of all persons who are members on that date; and
  2. Of persons who have ceased to be members since the last return, with the number of shares held by each member and details of share transfers since the last return.

The summary:

  1. The amount of the share capital of the company and the number of shares into which it is divided;
  2. The number of shares taken from the commencement of the company up to the date of the return;

Question 5 (14 Marks)

a) What is a partnership at will? (2)
b) Explain the different kinds of partnership with examples. (5)
c) "A partner is an agent of the firm." – Explain the statement with reference to the relations of partners to third parties under the Partnership Act, 1932. (7)

5. a) What is a partnership at will? (2)

Answer: As per Section 7 of the Partnership Act 1932, where no provision is made by contract between the partners for the duration of their partnership, or for the determination (termination) of their partnership, the partnership is called a "partnership at will." Two conditions must be fulfilled: (1) no agreement fixing the period of the partnership, and (2) no clause regarding its determination. In such a partnership, a partner may retire by simply giving notice to the other partners.

5. b) Explain the different kinds of partnership with examples. (5)

Answer: Kinds of partnership are based on two criteria:

A. With regard to duration:

  1. Partnership at Will (Sec. 7): No fixed period is prescribed for the expiration of the partnership, and no clause exists for its determination. It continues as long as the partners will, and can be dissolved by notice. Example: two friends open a grocery business with no time limit agreed.
  2. Partnership for a Fixed Period: The partners fix the duration of the firm; after expiry of that period the partnership ends. If the partners continue the business after expiry, it becomes a partnership at will. Example: a partnership formed for five years.

B. On the basis of the extent of business:

  1. Particular Partnership (Sec. 8): Created for completing a particular adventure, project or undertaking; it ends when the project is completed (though the partners may choose to continue). Example: a partnership formed only for constructing one building.
  2. General Partnership: Created for carrying on a business generally, with no particular task to be completed — the business is general in nature. Example: a firm carrying on a continuing trading business of all kinds.

5. c) "A partner is an agent of the firm." – Explain the statement with reference to the relations of partners to third parties under the Partnership Act, 1932. (7)

Answer: Sections 18 to 22 of the Partnership Act deal with the relations of partners to third parties, and they embody the principle of mutual agency, which is the true test of partnership.

  1. Partner as agent (Sec. 18): Every partner is an agent of the firm for the purposes of the business of the firm. A partner acts both as principal and agent — when he acts in his own interest he is a principal, and when he acts in the interest of another partner or the firm he is an agent. But, he is not an agent for dealings or transactions between the partners themselves.
  2. Implied authority (Sec. 19): Any act performed by a partner in the usual course of the firm's business binds the firm itself. This authority to bind the firm is called the implied authority of a partner.
  3. Extension or restriction of authority (Sec. 20): The partners may, by contract between them, extend or restrict the implied authority of any partner. A third party dealing without notice of the restriction is still protected.
  4. Acts in emergency (Sec. 21): An act done by a partner in an emergency, of the kind a prudent man would do to protect the firm from loss, binds the firm.
  5. Mode of doing the act (Sec. 22): To bind the firm, an act or instrument must be done or executed in the firm's name, or in a manner expressing or implying an intention to bind the firm.

So, because each partner can, within the scope of the firm's ordinary business, create rights and liabilities that bind all the other partners, a partner is truly an agent of the firm, and the firm is liable to third parties for the acts of every partner done within his actual or implied authority.

Question 6 (14 Marks)

a) Define delivery and document of title to goods under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. (2)
b) Explain the essential elements of a contract for the sale of goods. (6)
c) "The transfer of risk follows the transfer of property in goods." – Explain the statement by distinguishing sale from an agreement to sell. (6)

6. a) Define delivery and document of title to goods under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. (2)

Answer: Under Section 2 of the Sale of Goods Act 1930, "delivery" means the voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another. Delivery may be actual (goods physically handed to the buyer), constructive (transfer effected without change of custody, for example, by attornment or acknowledgment), or symbolic (delivery of a token, for example, handing over the key of a godown containing the goods).

"document of title to goods" includes a bill of lading, dock-warrant, warehouse keeper's certificate, wharfinger's certificate, railway receipt, warrant or order for the delivery of goods, and any other document used in the ordinary course of business as proof of the possession or control of goods, or authorizing (by endorsement or delivery) the possessor of the document to transfer or receive the goods thereby represented.

6. b) Explain the essential elements of a contract for the sale of goods. (6)

Answer: The essential elements of a contract of sale of goods are:

  1. Two parties: There must be a buyer — a person who buys or agrees to buy goods [Sec. 2(1)] — and a seller, who must be different persons, since a person cannot buy his own goods.
  2. Goods: The subject matter must be goods — every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money [Sec. 2(7)], including stock and shares, growing crops, and things attached to land agreed to be severed before sale. The goods may be existing, future or depending on an uncertain event.
  3. Transfer of property (ownership): The contract must contemplate the transfer of the general property (ownership) in the goods from the seller to the buyer — not merely possession or a special interest.
  4. Price — money consideration: The consideration must be money (the price). An exchange of goods for goods is barter, not sale; but a part-exchange of goods with money is a sale.
  5. Sale and agreement to sell both covered (Sec. 4): The contract may be an actual sale (property passes at once) or an agreement to sell (property passes at a future time or on fulfilment of a condition).
  6. Essential elements of a valid contract: As a species of contract, it must satisfy the requirements of the Contract Act 1872 — free consent, competent parties, lawful consideration and object. No particular form is required: it may be written, oral or implied.

6. c) "The transfer of risk follows the transfer of property in goods." – Explain the statement by distinguishing sale from an agreement to sell. (6)

Answer: The general rule (res perit domino) is that risk follows ownership — whoever owns the goods bears the loss if they are damaged or destroyed, regardless of who has possession. This is well illustrated by the distinction between a sale and an agreement to sell:

In a sale: The property in the goods passes to the buyer immediately at the time of the contract (executed contract (executed contract)). As a result, the transfer of risk also takes place immediately. If the goods are subsequently lost or destroyed, the loss falls on the buyer, even though the goods may still be in the possession of the seller. The buyer also obtains a right right in rem — a right to enjoy the goods against the whole world.

In an agreement to sell: The property passes only at a future time or upon fulfilment of certain conditions (contract yet to be completed (executory contract)). Since ownership has not been transferred, there is no transfer of risk — the loss of the goods falls on the seller, even though the goods may be in the possession of the buyer. The buyer has only a right right in personam against the seller.

Illustration: A sells his specific car to B for Tk. 10 lakh, the car remaining in A's garage until B collects it. The car is destroyed by fire without anyone's fault. Since this was a completed sale, ownership (and risk) had passed to B — B bears the loss and must pay the price. If, instead, A had merely agreed to sell the car after repainting it (a condition pending), ownership and risk would have remained with A, and the loss would fall on A.

This consequence also extends to insolvency situations: in a sale, the buyer can claim the goods from the seller's Receiver; in an agreement to sell he can claim only a share of money paid equally to creditors for the money paid — again reflecting who "owns" the goods when the event occurs. The parties may, however, agree otherwise, and risk may be separated from ownership by express contract.

Question 7 (14 Marks)

a) What is meant by carriage of goods? (2)
b) Identify the major laws relating to the carriage of goods by road, sea and air. (5)
c) Discuss the important provisions of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925. (7)

7. a) What is meant by carriage of goods? (2)

Answer: Carriage of goods means the transportation of goods from one place to another by land, sea or air under a contract of carriage. A carrier is a person or organization employed to carry goods or people, with or without remuneration, under an express or implied contract (for example, Bangladesh Railway, BRTC, shipping lines). A contract of carriage is a contract of bailment for reward (locatio operis faciendi) — a contract of hire of labor and services for moving goods.

7. b) Identify the major laws relating to the carriage of goods by road, sea and air. (5)

Answer: Goods can be carried by land, sea, or air, and different laws govern each mode:

  1. Carriage by land (road and rail): Governed by (i) The Carriers Act, 1865, which regulates common carriers — persons (other than the Government) engaged in transporting property for hire indiscriminately; and (ii) The Railways Act (Bangladesh Railway is regulated by its own statute, the Bangladesh Railway Act, 1890). These Acts are not exhaustive; where they are silent, courts resort to English Common Law. Private and gratuitous carriers are governed by the Contract Act, 1872 (as bailees).
  2. Carriage by sea: Governed by The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925, which extends to the whole of Bangladesh and deals with the liability, responsibility and rights relating to goods from loading until unloading; the bill of lading is the central document.
  3. Carriage by air: Governed by the Carriage by Air Act (giving effect to international conventions such as the Warsaw Convention), which regulates the liability of air carriers for goods, baggage and passengers in international and domestic carriage.

7. c) Discuss the important provisions of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925. (7)

Answer: The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 extends to the whole of Bangladesh and was enacted because transporting goods by ship was, and remains, a vital part of global trade. Its important provisions and features are:

  1. Application of the Rules (Sec. 2): Subject to the Act, the Rules set out in the Schedule (the Hague Rules) have effect in relation to the carriage of goods by sea in ships carrying goods from any port in Bangladesh to any other port, whether inside or outside Bangladesh.
  2. Scope — "tackle to tackle": The Act deals with the liability, responsibility, and rights of the parties in respect of the goods from the time the vessel begins loading until the goods are unloaded from the vessel.
  3. Contract of carriage: A contract of carriage by sea is made between a shipper and a carrier, by which the carrier undertakes, for a charge (freight), to transport the shipper's cargo to a destination and deliver it to a designated person. Often the agreement is initially verbal, following which the carrier issues a bill of lading upon shipment.
  4. The bill of lading: The central document under the Act, serving three functions — (i) a receipt recording the goods as loaded on board; (ii) a document of title to the goods, transferable by endorsement/delivery; and (iii) evidence of the contract of carriage between the parties.
  5. Responsibilities and liabilities: Contracts for carrying goods by ship, whether loading or handling, come under the rules of "responsibility and liability." The carrier must exercise due diligence to make the ship seaworthy, and properly load, handle, stow, carry and care for the goods.
  6. Quality checks and legality: The law restricts the export or import of illegal goods, and the quality of goods is checked at every step; the carrier is not liable if the quality of the goods was poor before loading of the consignment began.
  7. Immunities and limits: The Rules also confer defined protections from liability on the carrier (for example, perils of the sea, act of God) and limit liability, balancing the interests of shippers and carriers in international trade.

Question 2 (14 Marks)

a) What is free consent? (2)
b) When is consent said to be not free? Explain coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation and mistake in brief. (6)
c) Discuss the remedies available to an injured party for breach of contract, explaining liquidated and unliquidated damages with examples. (6)

2. a) What is free consent? (2)

Answer: According to Section 14 of the Contract Act 1872, two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense. Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by coercion (Sec. 15), undue influence (Sec. 16), fraud (Sec. 17), misrepresentation (Sec. 18), or mistake (Sec. 20). Free consent is one of the essential conditions of a valid contract under Section 10.

2. b) When is consent said to be not free? Explain coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation and mistake in brief. (6)

Answer: Consent is not free when it is obtained by any of the following factors, which generally render the contract voidable at the option of the injured party:

  1. **Coercion (Sec. 15): Committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Penal Code, or unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain any property, to the prejudice of any person, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement. Example: A threatens to harm B unless B sells his land at a low price.
  2. Undue Influence (Sec. 16): Where one party, by reason of a dominant position (real or apparent authority, trust relationship, or mental distress), obtains an unfair advantage over the other. Example: a spiritual adviser inducing a devotee to gift all property.
  3. Fraud (Sec. 17): Any of the following acts committed by a party with intent to deceive: suggestion of a false fact as true; active concealment of a fact; promise made without intention of performing it; any other act fitted to deceive; or any act declared fraud by law. Example: selling a car while concealing that its engine is defective.
  4. **Misrepresentation (Sec. 18): A positive assertion of a fact, made in good faith but without reasonable grounds, which is not true; or any breach of duty giving an advantage by misleading another; or innocently causing a party to make a mistake about the subject matter. Example: a seller honestly but wrongly stating that goods are of a certain grade.
  5. Mistake (Sec. 20): Where both parties are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void (not merely voidable). Example: A and B contract for the sale of a specific horse, but the horse was dead at the time of the contract, unknown to both.

2. c) Discuss the remedies available to an injured party for breach of contract, explaining liquidated and unliquidated damages with examples. (6)

Answer: When a party to a contract fails to perform his obligations, the injured party has the following remedies:

  1. Rescission: The injured party may cancel (rescind) the contract and refuse further performance.
  2. Damages: Monetary compensation for the loss suffered due to the breach.
  3. specific performance: A court order directing the failing party to perform the contract as agreed (available where damages are inadequate, for example, sale of unique property).
  4. injunction: A court order restraining a party from doing something in breach of the contract.
  5. Quantum meruit (reasonable payment for work done): A right to claim reasonable remuneration for work done when the contract is discharged before completion.

Liquidated damages: These are damages fixed in advance by the parties themselves in the contract at the time of formation, to be payable in the event of breach. They represent the parties' estimate of the probable loss. Example: A construction contract provides that if the contractor fails to complete the building by the agreed date, he shall pay Tk. 10,000 for each day of delay — this is liquidated damages.

Unliquidated damages: These are damages not fixed in advance in the contract; they are assessed and awarded by the court after the breach, based on the actual loss suffered by the injured party. The court places the injured party in the position he would have been in had the contract been performed. Example: A supplier fails to deliver goods and the buyer has to purchase from another source at a higher price — the difference in cost, plus any extra loss, is unliquidated damages assessed by the court.

The distinction is that liquidated damages are agreed in advance and need not be proved; unliquidated damages must be proved and calculated by the court based on actual injury.

Question 3 (14 Marks)

a) What is a cheque? (2)
b) What is crossing of a cheque? Explain its significance with examples. (5)
c) Explain the rights and liabilities of the drawer, drawee and payee of a negotiable instrument. (7)

3. a) What is a cheque? (2)

Answer: According to Section 6 of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, a cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a named bank and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand. It is a negotiable instrument with two special qualifications: (i) it is always drawn on a named bank, and (ii) it is always payable on demand. All cheques are bills of exchange, but all bills are not cheques. A cheque does not require acceptance.

3. b) What is crossing of a cheque? Explain its significance with examples. (5)

Answer: Crossing of a cheque involves drawing two parallel across lines across the face of the cheque, with or without additional words such as "account payee" or "not negotiable."

Significance:

  1. Additional security: Crossing restricts the transferability of the cheque and ensures that payment is made only through a banker, reducing the risk of theft or fraudulent encashment.
  2. "Account payee" crossing: When the words "account payee" are added, the cheque can be collected only for the account of the named payee, preventing transfer to unauthorized persons. Example: an employer crosses a salary cheque "account payee" in favour of the employee — only the employee's bank account can receive the payment.
  3. "Not negotiable" crossing: The holder obtains no better title than the transferor, protecting the drawer against claims by holders with defective title.
  4. General crossing: Two parallel lines without words direct the banker to pay only to another banker (for collection), not in cash over the counter.

Example: A draws a cheque for Tk. 50,000 in favour of B and crosses it with two parallel lines and writes "A/C Payee Only." B cannot cash it at the counter; it must be deposited into B's bank account, preventing misuse if the cheque is lost or stolen.

3. c) Explain the rights and liabilities of the drawer, drawee and payee of a negotiable instrument. (7)

Answer:

Drawer:

  • Rights: The drawer has the right to receive payment from the drawee according to the terms of the instrument; he can specify the place and mode of payment.
  • Liabilities: The drawer is secondarily liable — he must compensate the holder in case of dishonor if the drawee refuses to pay. In the case of a cheque, the drawer must ensure sufficient funds in his account. On dishonor by non-acceptance of a bill, the drawer can be sued immediately.

Drawee:

  • Rights: The drawee has the right to accept or refuse a bill of exchange. If he accepts, he becomes the acceptor and assumes primary liability for payment. In the case of a cheque, the drawee is always the banker on whom the cheque is drawn.
  • Liabilities: Upon acceptance, the drawee/acceptor is primarily liable to pay the amount on the due date. If he refuses to accept or pay, he must communicate the dishonor to the holder. In case of dishonor by non-payment, the drawee (bank) can be sued.

Payee:

  • Rights: The payee has the right to receive payment from the drawer or drawee, depending on the type of instrument. The payee can transfer these rights to another party through endorsement and delivery.
  • Liabilities: The payee is not usually liable for payment unless he has also signed as a co-drawer or co-endorser. But, if he endorses the instrument, he becomes an endorser and is secondarily liable if the instrument is dishonored. The payee is responsible for properly endorsing the instrument to transfer it.

4. a) What is an Annual General Meeting (AGM)? (2)

Answer: An Annual General Meeting (AGM) is a general meeting of the members of a company held once in each calendar year to transact ordinary business and review the affairs of the company. Under Section 81 of the Companies Act 1994, every company shall in each year hold, in addition to any other meetings, a general meeting as its AGM, and shall specify the meeting as such in the notices calling it.

4. b) State the rules relating to arranging an AGM of a company and the penalty for default in complying with them. (6)

Answer: Rules for arranging an AGM (Sec. 81):

  1. Every company must hold an AGM in each year of the Gregorian calendar.
  2. Not more than 15 months shall elapse between the date of one AGM and the next.
  3. A company may hold its first AGM within a period of not more than 18 months from the date of registration as a company; if held within that period, no AGM is necessary in the year of registration as a company or the following year.
  4. The Registrar may, on application within 30 days from expiry of the period, extend the time for holding any AGM (not being the first) by a period not exceeding 90 days or not exceeding 31st December of the calendar year in relation to which the AGM is required, whichever is earlier.
  5. If a company defaults in complying, the Court may, on application of any member, call or direct the calling of a general meeting and give other directions.

Penalty for default (Sec. 82): If default is made in holding an AGM in accordance with Section 81(1), or in complying with any Court direction under Section 81(2), the company and every officer in default shall be punishable with a fine up to Tk. 10,000, and in case of a continuing default, with a further fine of up to Tk. 250 for every day after the first day during which the default continues.

4. c) Discuss the rules for calling an extraordinary general meeting on requisition and the provisions regarding minutes of proceedings of general meetings. (6)

Answer: special general meeting (EGM) on Requisition (Sec. 84):

  1. The directors of a company with share capital must, on requisition of holders of not less than one-tenth of the issued share capital (on which all calls are paid), immediately proceed to call an extraordinary general meeting (EGM). For a company without share capital, requisition by members with not less than one-tenth of total voting power suffices.
  2. The requisition must state the objects of the meeting and be signed by the requisitionists and deposited at the registered office.
  3. If directors do not call the meeting within 21 days from deposit of the requisition, for a day not later than 45 days from deposit, the requisitionists (or a majority in value) may themselves call the meeting, but it must be held before the expiration of three months from the date of deposit.
  4. Any meeting so called shall be held in the same manner, as nearly as possible, as meetings called by directors.
  5. Reasonable expenses incurred by requisitionists due to the directors' failure shall be repaid by the company and retained out of fees/remuneration due to failing directors.

Minutes of Proceedings (Sec. 89):

  1. Every company shall cause minutes of all proceedings of general meetings and meetings of directors to be entered in books kept for that purpose.
  2. Any minute, if purporting to be signed by the chairman of the meeting or of the next succeeding meeting, shall be evidence of the proceedings.
  3. Until the contrary is proved, a meeting in respect of which minutes have been made shall be considered to have been properly called and held, and the proceedings described in the minutes shall be considered to have been properly held, and appointments of directors or liquidators at such meeting shall be considered valid.
  4. Books containing minutes of general meetings shall be kept at the registered office and be open to inspection by any member without charge during business hours (at least two hours each day).
  5. Any member may, after 14 days from the meeting, request a copy of any minutes and be furnished with it within seven days; default renders the company and every officer in default liable to a fine.

Question 5 (14 Marks)

a) Who is a minor partner? (2)
b) Explain partner by estoppel, nominal partner and sleeping partner with examples. (5)
c) "Agreement in restraint of trade is void, yet partners can restrain each other." – Explain the statement in light of the Partnership Act, 1932. (7)

5. a) Who is a minor partner? (2)

Answer: A minor (a person below the age of majority) cannot be a partner under the Contract Act 1872, since a minor is not competent to contract. But, under Section 30 of the Partnership Act 1932, a minor may be admitted to the benefits of partnership with the consent of all the existing partners. Such a minor partner shares profits but his liability is limited to his share of the firm's property — he is not personally liable for the firm's losses or obligations to third parties.

5. b) Explain partner by estoppel, nominal partner and sleeping partner with examples. (5)

Answer:

  1. Partner by Estoppel (Holding Out): A person who is not actually a partner but represents himself, by words or conduct, to another as a partner, cannot afterwards deny the partnership. He becomes liable as a partner to third parties who gave credit to the firm on the faith of such representation. Example: a retired partner who allows the firm to continue using his name without giving public notice of retirement.
  2. nominal partner: A partner to the firm only by name — he has no significant or real interest in the firm, contributes no capital, and takes no part in management, but lends his name and reputation to the business. Example: a well-known businessman whose name appears on the firm's letterhead to attract customers, without any real involvement.
  3. sleeping/dormant partner: A partner who does not participate in the conduct of the business but contributes capital, shares profits and losses, and is bound by the acts of all the other partners. Example: an investor who puts money into a firm but never attends to its daily affairs.

5. c) "Agreement in restraint of trade is void, yet partners can restrain each other." – Explain the statement in light of the Partnership Act, 1932. (7)

Answer: General rule — agreement in restraint of trade is void: Under Section 27 of the Contract Act 1872, every agreement by which any person is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void. The law favours free trade and considers that no person should be unreasonably prevented from earning a livelihood.

Exception for partners — Section 11 of the Partnership Act 1932: Section 11 provides a specific exception. Partners may, by their partnership deed, restrain each other from carrying on any business other than that of the firm during the continuance of the partnership. Such restraint must be contained in the partnership deed and must be reasonable in scope.

Reason for the exception: Partnership is founded on mutual trust and confidence. If one partner could freely compete with the firm while still being a partner, it would undermine the firm's business and betray the other partners' trust. The law therefore permits partners to agree not to engage in rival businesses during the partnership.

Illustration: A, B and C are partners in a law firm. Their deed provides that none of them shall practice law independently or with any other firm during the partnership. This restraint is valid under Section 11, even though a similar clause between unrelated persons would be void under Section 27 of the Contract Act.

Limitation: The restraint applies only during the continuance of the partnership and only as to businesses competing with the firm. A blanket prohibition on all trade by a partner would still be void.

Question 6 (14 Marks)

6. a) Who is a "buyer" and who is a "seller" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? (2)

Answer: Under Section 2 of the Sale of Goods Act 1930:

  • "Buyer" means a person who buys or agrees to buy goods.
  • "Seller" means a person who sells or agrees to sell goods.

Thus both terms cover persons who have entered into an actual sale as well as those who have merely agreed to sell or buy (an agreement to sell).

6. b) Distinguish between specific goods, identified and separated goods and unidentified and separated goods. (6)

Answer: All three are subtypes of existing goods — goods owned by or in the possession of the seller at the time of the contract:

  1. Specific Goods [Sec. 2(14)]: Goods that are "identified and agreed upon" at the time the contract of sale is formed. They are particular, designated goods. Example: you advertise your particular mobile phone online with its picture; a buyer agrees to buy that specific phone.
  2. Ascertained Goods: Not defined in the Act but recognized by judicial interpretation — specific goods that have been selected and separated from a larger set after the contract is made. Example: out of 500 apples in stock, you separate 200 particular apples to fulfil an order; those 200 are identified and separated goods.
  3. Unidentified and separated Goods: Goods that have not been specifically identified but are left to be selected from a larger group by the seller. Example: you agree to sell 200 apples out of your 500 without specifying which ones; the seller chooses any 200 from the lot.

Distinction: In specific goods, identification occurs at the time of the contract; in identified and separated goods, identification occurs after the contract by selection from a bulk; in unidentified and separated goods, identification is deferred and left to the seller's choice.

Question 2 (14 Marks)

a) Define contract as per Section 2(h) of the Contract Act, 1872. (2)
b) Explain the essential elements of a valid contract. (6)
c) Discuss the different types of contracts with examples and explain how each type affects the legal obligations of the parties involved. (6)

2. a) Define contract as per Section 2(h) of the Contract Act, 1872. (2)

Answer: Under Section 2(h) of the Contract Act 1872, a contract is defined as "an agreement enforceable by law." It is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties that creates enforceable rights and obligations, in which there is a promise to do something in return for a valuable benefit known as consideration.

2. b) Explain the essential elements of a valid contract. (6)

Answer: Under Section 10, the essential elements of a valid contract are:

  1. Offer and Acceptance: One party must make a clear offer and the other must accept it clearly and without doubt, creating a meeting of minds.
  2. Free Consent (Sec. 14): Consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake.
  3. Competent Parties (Sec. 11): Parties must be of majority age, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law.
  4. Lawful Consideration and Lawful Object (Sec. 23): Consideration and object must not be forbidden by law, fraud, immoral, or opposed to public policy.
  5. Intention to Create Legal Relations: Both parties must intend the agreement to have legal consequences (absent in social/domestic agreements).
  6. Certainty and Possibility of Performance: Terms must be clear and specific; the contract must be capable of performance.
  7. Not Expressly Declared Void: The agreement must not fall within categories declared void by law (for example, agreements in restraint of marriage, trade, legal proceedings; wagering agreements).
  8. Legal Formalities (where required): Some contracts must be in writing, registered, or attested (for example, contracts for sale of immovable property).

2. c) Discuss the different types of contracts with examples and explain how each type affects the legal obligations of the parties involved. (6)

Answer: Contracts may be classified as follows:

A. On the basis of formation:

  1. Express contracts: Terms stated in words (written or oral). Example: A orally agrees to sell his car to B for Tk. 5 lakh. Both parties are bound by the expressly stated terms; breach gives rise to remedies for the stated obligations.
  2. Implied contracts: Arising from conduct. Example: raising a paddle at an auction. The parties are bound by conduct-based obligations even without written terms.
  3. Quasi-contracts (Chapter V): Obligations imposed by law without agreement. Example: supply of necessaries to a minor (Sec. 68) — the supplier can recover from the minor's estate even though there was no contract.

B. On the basis of validity:

  1. Valid contracts: Fulfil all essentials of Section 10. Both parties are fully bound; the injured party can sue for breach. Example: a written employment contract.
  2. Void contracts (Sec. 2(j)): Unenforceable from inception. Neither party can sue; no legal obligations arise. Example: a contract with an impossible object.
  3. Voidable contracts: Valid until avoided by the injured party (consent not free). The cheated party may affirm or cancel. Example: a contract obtained by fraud — the victim may choose to enforce or cancel.
  4. Illegal contracts: Object or consideration forbidden by law. Unenforceable and may attract punishment; collateral transactions also also affected. Example: a contract for smuggling goods.
  5. Void-ab-initio contracts: Void from the very beginning. Example: a minor's contract (Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose).

C. On the basis of execution:

  1. Executed contracts: Fully performed. No further obligations remain.
  2. Executory contracts: Performance yet to be completed. Both parties remain bound to future performance.

Each type determines whether and how the parties can enforce obligations, claim damages, or escape liability.

Question 3 (14 Marks)

a) Define promissory note. (2)
b) Identify the parties to a promissory note and a cheque, and briefly state the role of each. (5)
c) What compensation rules apply on the dishonor of a negotiable instrument? Explain the position of the holder in case of dishonor by non-acceptance and by non-payment. (7)

3. a) Define promissory note. (2)

Answer: Under Section 4 of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, a "promissory note" is an instrument in writing (not being a bank-note or currency-note) containing an clear promise without condition, signed by the maker, to pay on demand or at a fixed or determinable future time a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of the instrument. Example: "I promise to pay B or order Taka 500."

3. b) Identify the parties to a promissory note and a cheque, and briefly state the role of each. (5)

Answer:

Parties to a Promissory Note:

  1. Maker: The person who promises to pay the amount. He is the principal debtor and primarily liable.
  2. Payee: The person to whom payment is promised.
  3. Holder: The person entitled to possession and to receive payment.
  4. Endorser: The holder who transfers the note by endorsement.
  5. Endorsee: The person to whom the note is endorsed.

Parties to a Cheque:

  1. Drawer: The person who draws (writes) the cheque on his bank account. He is secondarily liable on dishonor.
  2. Drawee (Banker): The bank on which the cheque is drawn. It is primarily liable to pay on showing the instrument for payment/acceptance (presentation for payment/acceptance).
  3. Payee: The person to whom the cheque is made payable.
  4. Holder: The person in possession entitled to receive payment.
  5. Endorser/Endorsee: As in a promissory note, if the cheque is transferred by endorsement.

3. c) What compensation rules apply on the dishonor of a negotiable instrument? Explain the position of the holder in case of dishonor by non-acceptance and by non-payment. (7)

Answer: Compensation rules: The compensation payable on dishonor equals the sum of:

  1. the amount due on the instrument;
  2. expenses on noting and protesting;
  3. interest on the principal amount; and
  4. the difference in exchange rate (if any).

Dishonor by Non-Acceptance (bills of exchange only):

  • Occurs when a bill is properly presented for acceptance but is not accepted within 48 hours, or the drawee is fictitious, incompetent, insolvent, dead, or gives qualified acceptance.
  • Position of holder: On dishonor by non-acceptance, the holder gets an immediate right to sue all prior parties (drawer and endorsers), even before maturity of the bill.
  • Drawee cannot be sued in case of dishonor by non-acceptance because he is not yet a party to the bill (he has not accepted). Only the drawer and endorsers are liable.

Dishonor by Non-Payment:

  • Occurs when the maker of a promissory note, the acceptor of a bill, or the drawee (bank) of a cheque defaults or refuses to pay on due showing the instrument for payment/acceptance (presentation for payment/acceptance).
  • Position of holder: The holder may sue all parties — in the case of a bill, the acceptor (drawee) is primarily liable and can be sued; the drawer and endorsers are secondarily liable.
  • The holder must give notice of dishonor within a reasonable time to all parties he wishes to hold liable (except the maker/acceptor, who are principal debtors). Failure to give notice discharges endorsers.

Key difference: On non-acceptance, the drawee cannot be sued; on non-payment, the drawee/acceptor (who has accepted or is the banker) can be sued as the principal debtor.

4. a) What is meant by a company limited by guarantee? (2)

Answer: A company limited by guarantee is one having the liability of its members limited by the memorandum to such amount as the members may respectively undertake to contribute to the assets of the company in the event of its being wound up. Such a company is also known as a "Guarantee Company." A pure guarantee company does not have a share capital — members guarantee a fixed sum rather than holding shares.

4. b) Briefly describe the different types of companies based on liability of members and mode of registration as a company. (7)

Answer:

A. Based on liability of members:

  1. Company Limited by Shares: Liability of each member is limited to the amount, if any, unpaid on the shares held by him. A shareholders cannot be called upon to pay more than the unpaid amount on his shares. Also known as a "Share Company."
  2. Company Limited by Guarantee: Liability limited to the amount members undertake to contribute to the assets on winding up. May or may not have a share capital.
  3. Unlimited Company: Liability of members is not limited — members have joint, several and unlimited obligations to meet any insufficiency in the company's assets on liquidation.

B. Based on mode of registration as a company:

  1. Chartered (Royal Chartered) Companies: Incorporated by royal charter or similar government instrument for trade, exploration or colonization. Example: East India Company (1600).
  2. Registered (Incorporated) Companies: Registered under the Companies Act of the country. In Bangladesh, companies are registered with RJSC under the Companies Act 1994.
  3. Unregistered Companies: Businesses not registered under the Companies Act — for example, sole proprietorships and ordinary partnerships. Not governed by company law.

  1. Public Limited Company: Minimum 7 members; shares publicly traded; greater heavy government rules.
  2. Private Limited Company: Minimum 2, maximum 50 members; shares not publicly traded; fewer regulations.
  3. One-Person Company: One member holds virtually the entire share capital.

4. c) What important books of account do companies require to keep as per Section 181 of the Companies Act, 1994? Also state the rules of penalty for not keeping them properly. (5)

Answer: Under Section 181, every company shall keep proper books of account with respect to:

a) all sums of money received and expended by the company and the matters in respect of which the receipt and expenditure take place; b) all sales and purchases of goods by the company; c) the assets and liabilities of the company; and d) in the case of a company engaged in production, distribution, marketing, transportation, processing, manufacturing, milling, extraction and mining activities, particulars relating to utilisation of material, labour and other items of overhead cost.

Rules: Books must give a true and fair view of the company's affairs; they must be kept at the registered office (or another place in Bangladesh for up to six months, with notice to the Registrar); branch offices must send summarised returns every three months; and books for at least 12 years must be preserved with relevant vouchers.

Penalty (Sec. 181(6)): If any person referred to in sub-section (7) — including managing director, executive director, general manager, manager, or every director where none of these officers exist — fails to take reasonable steps to secure following rules, or willfully causes default, he shall be punishable with imprisonment up to 6 months, or a fine up to Tk. 5,000, or both, for each offence.

  1. No company shall appoint or employ any individual as managing director for a term exceeding five years at a time.

  3. Re-appointment is permitted for a further period not exceeding five years on each occasion, but no re-appointment, re-employment or extension shall be made without the consent of the company in general meeting.

These provisions prevent concentration of managerial power indefinitely and ensure shareholders control over long-term management appointments.

Question 6 (14 Marks)

a) What is a particular partnership? (2)
b) "Partnership arises from contract and not from status." – Explain the statement with reference to the Partnership Act, 1932. (6)
c) Discuss the rights of a partner after dissolution of the firm and the rules regarding retirement and expulsion of a partner. (6)

6. a) What is a particular partnership? (2)

Answer: Under Section 8 of the Partnership Act 1932, a particular partnership is one in which a person becomes a partner with another in respect of a particular adventure or undertaking only. When that adventure or project is completed, the partnership comes to an end, though the partners may choose to continue the firm. Example: three persons partner together solely to construct one specific building.

6. b) "Partnership arises from contract and not from status." – Explain the statement with reference to the Partnership Act, 1932. (6)

Answer: Section 5 of the Partnership Act 1932 expressly states: "The relation of partnership arises from contract and not from status."

Meaning: Partnership can be created only by a voluntary agreement between persons — it cannot arise automatically from family status, inheritance, or operation of law.

Illustrations from the Act:

  1. Members of a Hindu joint family carrying on a family business as such are not partners, even though they share profits — their relation is one of status, not contract.
  2. A Burmese Buddhist husband and wife carrying on business as such are not partners — their relation arises from marital status.
  3. Co-owners of property who share rent are not necessarily partners unless they have agreed to carry on business as partners.

Why this matters: Because partnership arises from contract, the rules of the Contract Act (capacity, offer, acceptance, free consent) apply to partnership formation. But, the special rules of the Partnership Act (for example, regarding minors) govern where they differ. The intention of the parties to form a partnership is a question of fact in each case.

6. c) Discuss the rights of a partner after dissolution of the firm and the rules regarding retirement and expulsion of a partner. (6)

Answer: Rights after dissolution (Sec. 46):

After dissolution, every partner or his representative is entitled, as against all other partners, to have the firm's property applied in payment of the debts and liabilities of the firm, and then to have the surplus distributed among the partners or their representatives according to their rights. A partner also has the right to wind up the business of the firm (Sec. 46).

Retirement (Sec. 32):

  1. A partner may retire with the consent of all other partners, unless the partnership deed waives the requirement of consent.
  2. In a partnership at will, a partner may retire by giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to retire.
  3. On retirement, the retiring partner may be discharged from liability to third parties by an agreement; otherwise he remains liable for debts incurred before retirement until public notice is given.

Expulsion (Sec. 33):

  1. A partner may not be expelled from a firm by any majority of the partners except in exercise of a power conferred by express agreement between the partners.
  2. Expulsion must be for in good faith (in good faith) reasons and in the best interest of the business.
  3. The partner sought to be expelled must be given reasonable warning and an opportunity of explanation before expulsion.
  4. An expelled partner has the same rights as a retired partner regarding settlement of accounts and liability to third parties.

Question 7 (14 Marks)

a) What is an act of bankruptcy? (2)
b) Trace the historical development of bankruptcy law in Bangladesh leading to the enactment of the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (5)
c) Explain the effects of bankruptcy and the provisions relating to discharge of a bankrupt under the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (7)

7. a) What is an act of bankruptcy? (2)

Answer: An act of bankruptcy is an act done by a debtor which, together with his inability to pay debts, entitles a creditor (or the debtor himself) to initiate bankruptcy proceedings. Section 9 of the Bankruptcy Act 1997 lists nine specific acts of bankruptcy (for example, transfer of property to cheat creditors, failure to pay after statutory notice, imprisonment in execution of a decree). The debtor must be a person of majority who is not a lunatic, and his assets must be insufficient to meet all his debts.

7. b) Trace the historical development of bankruptcy law in Bangladesh leading to the enactment of the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (5)

Answer: The historical development is as follows:

  1. Ancient times: The condition of a debtor was pitiable — creditors could cut the debtor's body into pieces, imprison him, subject him to stripes and hard labour, or even sell him, his wife and children.
  2. Later stage: If the debtor ceded all his fortune to creditors, he was secured from jail — but this was still harsh.
  3. Recognition of bad luck: It was realized that not all defaults were deliberate; failure to pay might be due to bad luck. The law of insolvency/bankruptcy was enacted with three purposes: (a) protect the debtor from creditor trouble / pressure; (b) realize and fairly distribute assets; (c) discharge the debtor for a fresh start.
  4. Colonial era — Insolvency (Dacca) Act, 1909 and Insolvency Act, 1920: These were the laws in force in Bangladesh (then part of British India/Pakistan). They applied only to natural persons and specifically excluded legal persons like companies (Sec. 9 of the 1920 Act).
  5. Bankruptcy Act, 1997: Enacted and came into force on 1st August 1997, repealing both previous Acts. It re-enacted the law using the word "bankruptcy" instead of "insolvency." It mostly followed the old Acts, but also aimed to take stronger action against bank borrowers who cannot pay, and to extend debt recovery to companies and other legal persons.

7. c) Explain the effects of bankruptcy and the provisions relating to discharge of a bankrupt under the Bankruptcy Act, 1997. (7)

Answer: Effects of bankruptcy:

  1. From the date of legal declaration, the debtor becomes an "undischarged bankrupt" and his assets vest in the Receiver for distribution among creditors.
  2. The bankrupt is subject to personal disqualifications until discharge — for example, cannot be elected to Parliament or local bodies, cannot be appointed as a Judge, Magistrate, Receiver, or obtain loans from banks/financial institutions.
  3. The bankruptcy court has full power to decide all questions of title, priority, and distribution of property (Sec. 5).
  4. Creditors must prove their claims; distribution is made fairly without undue preference to any creditor.
  5. The bankrupt's after-acquired property may also be claimed for the benefit of creditors during the bankruptcy.

Discharge of bankrupt (Chapter V):

  1. Chapter V deals with discharge and applies only to individual bankrupts.
  2. The order of discharge releases the bankrupt from the burden of his pre-existing debts (except specified debts) and relieves him of personal disqualifications.
  3. After discharge, the term "bankrupt" cannot be applied to him; he gets a fresh start.
  4. An absolute order of discharge (i) releases the bankrupt from all provable debts, and (ii) removes personal disqualifications (for example, ability to hold certain posts).
  5. Exceptions — discharge does NOT release the bankrupt from: (a) debts due to the Government; (b) debts incurred by fraud or breach of trust; (c) debts obtained by fraud; and (d) maintenance orders in favour of wife or children.
  6. The bankrupt must meet these excepted obligations from after-acquired property (Sec. 51).
  7. Discharge does not terminate proceedings as to remaining assets — the court retains power to direct distribution and redistribution if errors occurred.
  8. A creditor who failed to prove his claim before discharge may still prove it after discharge if assets remain in the Receiver's hands.

Question 1 (14 Marks)

a) What is a contract of agency? (2)
b) Explain the essential features of a contract of agency. (6)
c) Discuss the duties of an agent towards the principal under the Contract Act, 1872. (6)

1. a) What is a contract of agency? (2)

Answer: A contract of agency is a contract by which one person (the agent) is employed by another (the principal) to do any act for him, or to represent him in dealings with third persons. Under Section 182 of the Contract Act 1872, an agent is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons; the person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the principal. No consideration is necessary to create an agency (Sec. 185).

1. b) Explain the essential features of a contract of agency. (6)

Answer: The essential features of agency are:

  1. Two parties — principal and agent: There must be a principal who authorizes, and an agent who acts on his behalf.
  2. Competence: The principal must be competent to contract (Sec. 183). Any person — even a minor — may become an agent, but a minor agent is not personally responsible to the principal (Sec. 184).
  3. Authority to act: The agent must have authority — express or implied — to act for the principal in dealings with third parties.
  4. Representative character: The agent represents the principal; contracts made within authority bind the principal, not the agent personally (subject to exceptions).
  5. No consideration necessary (Sec. 185): Agency may be created without consideration.
  6. Fiduciary relationship: The relationship is based on trust and confidence; the agent must act in good faith for the principal's interest.
  7. Intention to create agency: Mere assistance or advice does not create agency; there must be an intention that the agent should represent the principal.

1. c) Discuss the duties of an agent towards the principal under the Contract Act, 1872. (6)

Answer: The main duties of an agent towards the principal include:

  1. Duty to follow instructions (Sec. 211): The agent must conduct the business of the agency according to the principal's directions; if none, according to the custom of that business.
  2. Duty of skill and care (Sec. 212): The agent must act with the skill and diligence ordinarily expected; he is liable for loss caused by his negligence or want of skill.
  3. Duty to render accounts (Sec. 213): The agent must render proper accounts to the principal on demand.
  4. Duty to communicate (Sec. 214): In cases of difficulty, the agent must use all reasonable diligence to communicate with the principal and seek instructions.
  5. Duty not to deal on his own account (Secs. 215–216): Without the principal's consent, the agent must not deal on his own account in the business of the agency; otherwise the principal may repudiate or claim benefits.
  6. Duty not to make secret profit: Any secret profit or bribe must be accounted for to the principal.
  7. Duty not to delegate (Sec. 190): Delegatus non potest delegare — an agent generally cannot appoint a sub-agent, except where custom, nature of work, or the principal's consent allows it.
  8. Duty to pay sums received (Sec. 218): The agent must pay to the principal all sums received on the principal's account, subject to lawful deductions.

Question 2 (14 Marks)

a) What is meant by termination (discharge) of a contract? (2)
b) How is a contract terminated? Discuss the different modes of discharge of a contract. (7)
c) Explain discharge of a contract by impossibility of performance (frustration) with suitable examples. (5)

2. a) What is meant by termination (discharge) of a contract? (2)

Answer: Termination (or discharge) of a contract means that the contractual relationship between the parties comes to an end, and the parties are freed from further performance of their obligations under the contract. After discharge, neither party can sue the other for further performance of the original contract (though rights already accrued, or remedies for earlier breach, may still survive).

2. b) How is a contract terminated? Discuss the different modes of discharge of a contract. (7)

Answer: A contract may be terminated (discharged) in the following main ways:

  1. By performance: When both parties fully perform their obligations according to the terms of the contract, the contract is discharged. Performance may be actual or attempted (tender of performance under Sec. 38).
  2. By mutual agreement / consent: The parties may end the contract by:
    • - Novation (Sec. 62): Substituting a new contract for the old one (new parties or new terms);
    • - Rescission: Cancelling the contract by mutual consent;
    • - Alteration: Changing the terms with mutual consent;
    • - Remission (Sec. 63): Accepting a lesser performance or waiving rights;
    • - Merger: Absorbing a lesser right into a higher right.
  3. By breach of contract: When one party fails or refuses to perform, the injured party may treat the contract as discharged and claim remedies (damages, rescission, specific performance, etc.). Breach may be actual or anticipatory.
  4. By impossibility / frustration (Sec. 56): When performance becomes impossible or unlawful after the contract is made, without the fault of either party, the contract becomes void.
  5. By operation of law: For example, death or insolvency of a party in contracts involving personal skill; merger of rights; unauthorized material alteration of a written contract.
  6. By lapse of time: Where the Limitation Act bars the remedy after the prescribed period, the right to enforce the contract is effectively lost (though the underlying obligation may still exist in some cases).
  7. By accord and satisfaction: The parties agree that a different act or payment will satisfy the original obligation, and that act is performed.

Thus, a contract does not last forever; it ends when obligations are fulfilled, the parties agree to end it, the law treats it as ended, or breach/impossibility releases the parties from further performance.

2. c) Explain discharge of a contract by impossibility of performance (frustration) with suitable examples. (5)

Answer: Under Section 56 of the Contract Act 1872:

  • An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void.
  • A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible or unlawful by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.

This is often called the doctrine of frustration — the foundation of the contract is destroyed, and further performance is excused.

Examples:

  1. Destruction of subject matter: A contracts to sell a specific horse to B. Before delivery, the horse dies without A's fault. The contract is discharged.
  2. Change of law: A agrees to import goods that later become prohibited by law. Performance becomes unlawful and the contract is void.
  3. Death or incapacity in personal contracts: A agrees to paint B's portrait; A dies before painting. The contract is discharged because it depended on A's personal skill.
  4. Non-occurrence of an expected event: A hall is hired for a concert on a particular day; the hall is destroyed by fire before that day. The contract is frustrated.

Note: Mere commercial hardship, rise in prices, or difficulty does not amount to impossibility. If impossibility is caused by the promisor's own fault, he remains liable for breach.

Answer: A certificate of commencement of business is a certificate issued by the Registrar after a public limited company has completed certain post-incorporation formalities (such as raising minimum subscription and filing the required declaration/documents).   4. Foreign shareholding (if any): If there is foreign investment, open a bank account in the proposed name, remit capital from abroad, and obtain an Encashment Certificate.

  1. Obtain a certificate of commencement of business before starting operations.
  2. Hold a statutory meeting (Sec. 83) within not less than one month and not more than six months from entitlement to commence business, and circulate the statutory report.
  3. Issue a prospectus (or statement in lieu of prospectus) when inviting public subscription — not required for a private company.
  4. Allow free transferability of shares (subject to law); private companies restrict transfer.
  5. Have at least 3 directors (private: at least 2) and generally appoint a company secretary (mandatory for public companies).

5. a) What is meant by a "stipulation" in a contract of sale of goods? (2)

Answer: A stipulation in a contract of sale is a term or provision of the contract relating to the goods. Under the Sale of Goods Act 1930, every stipulation may be either a condition or a warranty, depending on whether it is essential to the main purpose of the contract or only collateral to it (Sec. 12). Whether a stipulation is a condition or a warranty depends on the construction of the contract; a stipulation may be a condition though called a warranty in the contract.

Answer:

5. c) When may a breach of condition be treated as a breach of warranty? Explain with examples. (6)

Answer: Under Section 13 of the Sale of Goods Act 1930, a breach of condition may be treated as a breach of warranty in the following cases:

  1. Voluntary waiver by the buyer: Where the buyer waives the condition, he may treat the breach as a breach of warranty and claim damages only, without rejecting the goods.
  2. Election by the buyer: Where the buyer elects to treat the breach of condition as a breach of warranty — for example, he accepts the goods knowing of the defect and sues only for damages.
  3. Acceptance of goods (Sec. 13(2)): Where a contract of sale is not severable and the buyer has accepted the goods (or part of them), the breach of any condition to be fulfilled by the seller can only be treated as a breach of warranty, and not as a ground for rejecting the goods and treating the contract as repudiated — unless there is a term of the contract to the contrary.
  4. Impossibility of rejection in certain cases: Where the goods have been so mixed, used, or dealt with that rejection is no longer practical, the buyer is confined to damages.

Examples:

  • A orders specific machinery. On delivery he finds it does not match the description (breach of condition), but he still installs and uses it. He has accepted the goods; he may claim damages as for breach of warranty, but generally cannot reject.
  • B buys cloth by sample. The bulk differs from the sample. If B accepts the cloth after inspection, the condition as to sample is treated as a warranty, and B's remedy is damages.

Exception: This rule does not affect cases where the condition is excused by law, or where the contract expressly preserves the right to reject despite acceptance.

6. a) What is arbitration? (2)

Answer: Arbitration is a method of settling disputes outside the ordinary courts, in which the parties agree to refer their dispute to one or more neutral persons (arbitrators) whose decision (the award) is binding on them. In Bangladesh, arbitration is governed mainly by the Arbitration Act (as referred to in the course materials). It is a form of alternative dispute resolution (ADR) based on party agreement.

6. b) Explain the importance and uses of arbitration in resolving business disputes. (5)

Answer: Arbitration is important for business because:

  1. Speed and lower cost: It is generally faster and less expensive than full court litigation.
  2. Privacy and confidentiality: Hearings are private, which protects trade secrets and business reputation.
  3. Party autonomy: Parties can choose arbitrators with commercial or technical expertise, the place of arbitration, and often the procedure.
  4. Enforceability: A valid arbitration award is binding and enforceable; courts generally support arbitration agreements by referring parties to arbitration.
  5. International trade: Cross-border contracts commonly use arbitration to avoid unfamiliar foreign courts and to obtain a neutral forum.
  6. Business continuity: Quicker settlement helps preserve commercial relationships and reduces uncertainty.

Thus, mercantile law supports arbitration as a practical tool for commercial dispute resolution.

6. c) What is meant by intellectual property? Briefly explain patents, trademarks and copyrights and their importance to business. (7)

Answer: Intellectual property (IP) means legal rights over creations of the mind — inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, symbols, names and images used in commerce. IP laws give the owner exclusive rights for a period, so others cannot exploit the creation without permission.

Main types:

  1. Patents: Protect new inventions and technological innovations. The patent holder gets an exclusive right to make, use and sell the invention for a limited period. Importance: Encourages research and development; allows firms to recover investment in innovation.
  2. Trademarks: Protect brand names, logos, slogans and other marks that distinguish goods or services of one business from another. Importance: Builds brand identity and customer goodwill; prevents passing off and unfair competition.
  3. Copyrights: Protect original literary, artistic, musical and dramatic works, and often computer software. Importance: Protects creative content, publications, designs and software products from unauthorized copying.

Importance to business overall:

  • Attracts investment by securing exclusive commercial rights;
  • Prevents competitors from free-riding on another's innovation or brand;
  • Supports licensing and franchising income;
  • Strengthens market position and consumer trust.

Mercantile law therefore treats IP protection as essential for innovation, fair competition and economic growth.

Answer: An e-contract (electronic contract) is a contract made through electronic means — such as websites, email, online marketplaces or electronic data interchange — where the parties usually do not meet in person. Examples include withdrawing money from an ATM, buying goods from an online shopping site, or bidding in an online auction.

7. b) Are e-contracts valid under the Contract Act, 1872? Explain with essentials. (5)

Answer: The Contract Act 1872 does not expressly use the term "e-contract," but it also does not prohibit such contracts. Courts have recognized e-contracts as valid if they satisfy the essentials of a valid contract under Section 10, namely:

  1. lawful offer and acceptance (which may be electronic);
  2. free consent;
  3. competent parties;
  4. lawful consideration and lawful object;
  5. not expressly declared void; and
  6. intention to create legal relations, certainty, and possibility of performance.

Special care is needed regarding free consent, because online terms may be one-sided or not properly brought to the other party's notice. Where consent is obtained by misrepresentation, fraud, or unfair concealment of terms, the contract may be voidable. Subject to these safeguards, an e-contract that meets Section 10 is as enforceable as a traditional paper contract.
